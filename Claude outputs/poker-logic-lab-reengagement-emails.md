## The one decision left: the make-good

The image sets up "bad hand," so the reader expects a payoff. An apology with nothing behind it reads thin. Three options, ranked.

**A. Extend the guarantee to 30 days for this list. My recommendation.**

> *You get 30 days to get your money back on this one instead of the usual 14. If it is not worth $24.99 to you, just reply and I will refund it, no questions.*

Costs nothing unless someone actually refunds, and it answers the real objection for a $25 product from a stranger, which is risk rather than price. It does not teach your list to wait for a coupon.

**Before using this:** your `/refunds` page says 14 days. Either update it, or word this as a one-time exception for this email so the two do not contradict each other. And you have to honor it, because a guarantee you argue about is worse than no guarantee at all.

**B. A time-boxed discount.**

> *Use POKER10 for $10 off through [DATE]. After that it goes back to $24.99.*

Stronger short-term pull. But it cuts a $23.63 net sale to about $13.63, and you need roughly one sale per five leads just to break even on the $4.85 you already paid for each of them. It also teaches this list that waiting gets a better price. **Only put a real date on it and actually turn it off**, or you burn the credibility the honest email just bought you.

**C. Nothing but the apology and the fix.**

Cleanest, and it gives you the truest read on what these leads convert at. Weakest response rate.

I would run A. If it underperforms, B becomes the follow-up rather than the opener.

## What to watch

Your list is healthy: 97% delivered, 29% opened, 11% clicked, zero complaints. What it has never had is a working destination, so treat this as your first real read on whether these leads convert at all.

Watch click-to-purchase, not opens. `InitiateCheckout` and `Purchase` both fire now, so for the first time you can see the whole path: clicked the email, reached pricing, started checkout, paid. Clicks that reach pricing but never start checkout mean the offer or the price is wrong. Checkouts that start and do not finish mean PayPal is the friction, which is the argument for putting card fields on your own page.

**What I would A/B test:** the subject line. "Sorry, we dealt you a bad hand" is candid and pairs with the image. "We had 7-2 offsuit and played it anyway" is funnier and more in-group, and it will either outperform badly or flop, which is worth knowing about this audience.
